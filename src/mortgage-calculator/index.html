How Does a UK Mortgage Work?

Amortisation Explained

Most UK mortgages use capital repayment (also called amortisation). Each monthly payment covers both the interest charged on your outstanding loan balance and a portion of the capital you borrowed. In the early years, most of your payment goes towards interest. Over time, as the loan balance shrinks, more of each payment chips away at the capital.

This is why even small overpayments early in your mortgage can have an outsized impact — they reduce the balance that future interest is calculated on, creating a compounding saving effect.

Why Overpay?

  • Save thousands in interest — Overpaying by just £100/month on a £200,000 mortgage at 5% could save you over £30,000 in interest.
  • Become mortgage-free sooner — Regular overpayments can slash years off your term, giving you financial freedom earlier.
  • Reduce your LTV — A lower loan-to-value ratio unlocks better remortgage rates when your fixed term ends.
  • Beat savings rates — Overpaying a 5% mortgage is equivalent to earning 5% tax-free on savings — often better than any savings account.

Important Considerations

Most UK lenders allow you to overpay up to 10% of the outstanding balance per year without an early repayment charge (ERC). Check your mortgage terms before making large overpayments. If you have other high-interest debts (like credit cards), it usually makes sense to clear those first.

Some borrowers prefer an offset mortgage, where your savings reduce the balance you pay interest on while keeping your cash accessible. Others choose interest-only mortgages (common for buy-to-let), where you only pay the interest each month and repay the capital at the end of the term.
