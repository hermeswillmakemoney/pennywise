Understanding Rental Yield

Gross Yield vs Net Yield

Gross yield is the simplest measure: annual rent divided by the property price, expressed as a percentage. It doesn't account for any costs, so it's a useful quick comparison tool but can be misleading. A £200,000 property renting at £1,000/month has a 6% gross yield — sounds great, but the real return may be much lower after costs.

Net yield deducts all your running costs from the rental income before calculating the percentage return. This includes mortgage interest, insurance, maintenance, service charges, ground rent, and void periods (when the property sits empty between tenancies). Net yield gives you a much more realistic picture of your actual return.

What Costs Should You Expect?

  • Mortgage interest — Most buy-to-let mortgages are interest-only. At current rates (4–6%), a 75% LTV mortgage on a £250,000 property costs roughly £700–£950/month in interest alone.
  • Landlord insurance — Typically £200–£400/year. Covers buildings, liability, and loss of rent.
  • Maintenance & repairs — Budget at least 0.5–1% of the property value per year. On a £250,000 property, that's £1,250–£2,500.
  • Service charges & ground rent — Leasehold properties come with annual charges that can range from a few hundred to several thousand pounds, especially in modern apartment blocks.
  • Void periods — Assume 2–4 weeks per year where the property is empty between tenancies. In a £1,100/month rental, 3 weeks' void costs you ~£760 in lost income.
  • Letting agent fees — If you use an agent, expect 8–15% of rent for full management, or a one-off fee for tenant-find only. Many landlords self-manage to save this cost.

UK Tax Implications for Landlords

Since April 2020, mortgage interest tax relief has been restricted for individual landlords. You can no longer deduct mortgage interest as a business expense — instead, you receive a 20% tax credit on your mortgage interest payments. This means higher-rate taxpayers (40% or 45%) only get basic-rate relief on their mortgage costs, significantly reducing net returns.

Rental income is added to your other income and taxed at your marginal rate (20%, 40%, or 45% in England, Wales & Northern Ireland; different bands apply in Scotland). You can still deduct other allowable expenses including insurance, maintenance, service charges, ground rent, and letting agent fees. Keep detailed records — HMRC expects you to report rental income via Self Assessment each year.

What's a Good Rental Yield?

  • 6%+ net yield — Excellent. You're likely in a high-demand, lower-price area (common in the North of England, Scotland, and Wales).
  • 4–6% net yield — Solid. Covers costs with a reasonable profit margin. Typical for most UK regions.
  • 2–4% net yield — Marginal. You may be relying heavily on capital appreciation. Common in London and the South East where property prices are high relative to rents.
  • Below 2% net yield — Risky. One major repair or extended void could wipe out years of profit. Consider whether capital growth justifies the low income return.
